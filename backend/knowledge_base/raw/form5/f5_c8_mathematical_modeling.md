# Topic: Mathematical Modeling
**Form:** Form 5 | **Chapter:** Chapter 8 | **Curriculum:** SPM KSSM

## Overview
Mathematical modeling is the process of using mathematics to represent, analyse and solve real-world problems. In this chapter, students learn how to identify a problem, make assumptions, define variables, apply suitable mathematics, verify solutions, refine models and report findings. Students also learn how linear, quadratic and exponential functions can be used as mathematical models.

## Concept: Mathematical Model
A mathematical model is a mathematical representation of a real-world situation, system or scenario.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- A mathematical model uses symbols, equations, graphs, tables or diagrams.
- It helps us understand a real-world situation.
- It can be used to make predictions.
- It may involve exact answers or approximate answers.
- A model is only valid under certain assumptions.

### Examples:
- Simple interest:

$$
I = Prt
$$

- Direct variation:

$$
y = kx
$$

- Compound interest:

$$
A(t)=P(1+r)^t
$$

- Quadratic model:

$$
y=ax^2+bx+c
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A model is not always perfect.
- A model depends on assumptions.
- If assumptions change, the model may need to be changed.
- Real-world problems may have more than one valid model.

## Concept: Mathematical Modeling
Mathematical modeling is the process of translating a real-world problem into a mathematical problem, solving it, then interpreting the answer back in the real-world context.

**Key Idea:**
Real-world problem $\rightarrow$ Mathematical model $\rightarrow$ Mathematical solution $\rightarrow$ Real-world interpretation

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not stop after calculating the answer.
- Always explain what the answer means in the real situation.
- Check whether the answer is reasonable.
- Open-ended problems may have different valid answers depending on assumptions.

## Concept: Problem Solving vs Mathematical Modeling
Problem solving and mathematical modeling are related, but they are not exactly the same.

| Item | Problem Solving | Mathematical Modeling |
|---|---|---|
| Information given | Usually complete | Often incomplete |
| Answer | Usually one correct answer | May have several possible answers |
| Method | Direct calculation | Requires assumptions and interpretation |
| Real-world complexity | Usually simplified | More realistic and open-ended |

### Example of Problem Solving:
A town has $40\,000$ people. If $45\%$ recycle plastic bottles and each person uses $5$ bottles per week, find the number of bottles recycled.

This can be solved directly using percentage.

### Example of Mathematical Modeling:
How long does it take to clean up an oil spill in the ocean?

This needs assumptions such as:
- Size of oil spill.
- Number of workers.
- Weather condition.
- Cleaning technology.
- Rate of cleaning.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Mathematical modeling questions usually require explanation, not only calculation.
- If the question is open-ended, state assumptions clearly.
- Different assumptions can lead to different models.

## Concept: Components of Mathematical Modeling
There are six main components in mathematical modeling.

### 1. Identifying and Defining the Problem
Understand what needs to be solved.

### 2. Making Assumptions and Identifying Variables
Simplify the real situation and define unknown quantities.

### 3. Applying Mathematics to Solve the Problem
Use suitable formulas, functions, graphs or calculations.

### 4. Verifying and Interpreting the Solution
Check whether the mathematical answer makes sense in the real-world context.

### 5. Refining the Mathematical Model
Improve the model if the assumptions are too simple or not realistic.

### 6. Reporting the Findings
Present the model, solution, interpretation, limitations and conclusion.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not skip assumptions.
- Variables must be clearly defined.
- A good report should include both calculations and explanation.
- Refining the model means improving it, not simply repeating the same answer.

## Concept: Mathematical Modeling Process
The modeling process is usually repeated until the model is suitable.

### Process:
1. Start with a real-world problem.
2. Identify and define the problem.
3. Make assumptions and identify variables.
4. Apply mathematics.
5. Verify and interpret the solution.
6. Decide whether the model needs refinement.
7. Refine if necessary.
8. Report the findings.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Mathematical modeling is not always a straight-line process.
- You may need to return to earlier steps.
- If the model does not fit the real situation, refine it.
- The final answer should include limitations of the model.

## Concept: Identifying and Defining the Problem
This step means understanding what the question is asking and what information is needed.

### Questions to Ask:
- What is the real-world issue?
- What needs to be calculated or predicted?
- What information is already given?
- What information is missing?
- What data should be collected?

### Example:
A store farther from home sells rice cheaper than a nearby store.

The problem is:

Should a person drive farther to buy cheaper rice?

Important information:
- Price of rice at store A.
- Price of rice at store B.
- Distance between stores.
- Petrol price.
- Petrol consumption rate.
- Number of rice packs to buy.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not start calculating before understanding the problem.
- Identify the goal clearly.
- For real-world problems, some information may need to be estimated or researched.

## Concept: Making Assumptions
Assumptions are statements accepted as true to simplify a real-world problem.

### Example Assumptions:
For the rice-store problem:
- The route between the house, store A and store B is a straight line.
- The person drives a car.
- The same brand and number of rice packs are bought from both stores.
- Petrol price is constant.
- Petrol consumption rate is constant.

### Why Assumptions Are Needed:
- Real life is complex.
- Not all information is available.
- Assumptions make the problem solvable.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Assumptions must be reasonable.
- Do not make assumptions that contradict the question.
- State assumptions clearly before forming the model.
- If assumptions are unrealistic, the model may not be reliable.

## Concept: Identifying Variables
A variable is a quantity with an unknown or changeable value.

### Example Variables:
In the rice-store model:

| Variable | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $s$ | Distance between store A and store B |
| $P_1$ | Price of one pack of rice at store A |
| $P_2$ | Price of one pack of rice at store B |
| $m$ | Petrol consumption rate in km per litre |
| $n$ | Number of rice packs bought |
| $H$ | Petrol price in RM per litre |
| $S$ | Money saved from cheaper rice |
| $T$ | Extra petrol cost |

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Define every variable used.
- Use suitable units.
- Do not use the same symbol for two different quantities.
- Variables should match the real-world meaning.

## Concept: Applying Mathematics
This step means forming and solving the mathematical model.

### Common Mathematical Tools:
- Linear functions.
- Quadratic functions.
- Exponential functions.
- Direct variation.
- Simple interest.
- Compound interest.
- Ratios.
- Graphs.
- Tables.
- Simultaneous equations.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Choose a model that fits the pattern of the data.
- Use correct units.
- Show substitution clearly.
- Approximate answers may be acceptable in modeling.

## Concept: Verifying and Interpreting the Solution
Verification means checking whether the answer is correct and reasonable.

Interpretation means explaining what the answer means in context.

### Questions to Ask:
- Does the answer solve the original problem?
- Is the answer realistic?
- Does the answer follow the assumptions?
- Is the model suitable for the real-world situation?
- Can the answer be negative? If yes, does that make sense?

### Example:
If a model says a person saves RM$2.50$ by driving farther to buy rice, the calculation may be correct.

But we must still ask:
- Is saving RM$2.50$ worth the extra time?
- Is the extra petrol cost estimated correctly?
- Is it environmentally reasonable to drive farther?
- Does the model consider traffic?

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A mathematically correct answer may still be unrealistic.
- Always interpret the answer using the context.
- State whether the model is suitable or limited.

## Concept: Refining the Mathematical Model
Refining means improving the model when the original assumptions are too simple.

### Example:
Original assumption:
- Store A and store B are on the same straight road.

Refined assumption:
- The stores are not on the same straight line.
- Actual road distance should be used instead of straight-line distance.
- Traffic, parking, toll and time can be included.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Refining does not mean the first model is completely wrong.
- It means making the model more realistic.
- New assumptions may require a new formula.
- More data can improve the model.

## Concept: Reporting the Findings
Reporting means presenting the complete modeling process and conclusion.

### A Good Report Includes:
1. Problem statement.
2. Assumptions.
3. Variables.
4. Mathematical model.
5. Calculations.
6. Interpretation.
7. Limitations.
8. Refinement if needed.
9. Final conclusion.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not report only the final answer.
- Include diagrams or symbols if they make the model clearer.
- Mention strengths and weaknesses of the model.
- Explain the conclusion in simple real-world language.

## Concept: Linear Function Model
A linear function model has a graph that resembles a straight line.

### General Form:
$$
y=mx+c
$$

or, for direct variation:

$$
y=kx
$$

### When to Use:
Use a linear model when:
- The quantity increases or decreases at a constant rate.
- The graph is approximately a straight line.
- One quantity varies directly with another.

### Example Situations:
- Petrol used and distance travelled.
- Temperature decrease at a constant rate.
- Simple interest over time.
- Cost based on number of items.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Linear models assume a constant rate.
- Not all real-world situations remain linear forever.
- Always check whether the straight-line assumption is reasonable.

## Concept: Direct Variation Model
In a direct variation model, one quantity is proportional to another.

### Formula:
$$
y=kx
$$

where $k$ is a constant.

### Worked Example:
Amin travels $405$ km using $45$ litres of petrol. Estimate the petrol needed for $198$ km.

Let:
- $x$ = distance travelled.
- $y$ = petrol needed.

Since petrol used varies directly with distance:

$$
y=kx
$$

Substitute:

$$
45=k(405)
$$

$$
k=\frac{45}{405}
$$

$$
k=\frac{1}{9}
$$

So:

$$
y=\frac{1}{9}x
$$

When:

$$
x=198
$$

$$
y=\frac{1}{9}(198)
$$

$$
y=22
$$

Therefore, $22$ litres of petrol are needed.

### Verifying the Model:
This model may not be suitable if:
- One route is through traffic.
- One route is highway driving.
- Driving speed is different.
- Road condition is different.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- State assumptions such as same driving condition.
- Direct variation gives a linear model through the origin.
- Interpret the answer in litres, km, RM or suitable units.

## Concept: Rice-Store Savings Model
This model compares savings from cheaper rice with extra petrol cost.

### Variables:
| Variable | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $P_1$ | Price of rice at store A |
| $P_2$ | Price of rice at store B |
| $n$ | Number of packs bought |
| $s$ | Extra one-way distance |
| $m$ | Petrol consumption rate |
| $H$ | Petrol price |
| $S$ | Savings from rice price difference |
| $T$ | Extra petrol cost |

### Savings from Rice:
$$
S=(P_1-P_2)n
$$

### Extra Petrol Cost:
$$
T=\frac{s}{m}\times H \times 2
$$

The factor $2$ is used because the journey is two-way.

### Decision Rule:
If:

$$
S>T
$$

then it is worth travelling farther.

If:

$$
S<T
$$

then it is not worth travelling farther.

### Worked Example:
Store A price:

$$
P_1=25.95
$$

Store B price:

$$
P_2=23.99
$$

Number of packs:

$$
n=2
$$

Savings:

$$
S=(25.95-23.99)(2)
$$

$$
S=3.92
$$

If:

$$
s=6,\quad m=17.6,\quad H=2.08
$$

then:

$$
T=\frac{6}{17.6}\times2.08\times2
$$

$$
T=1.42
$$

Since:

$$
3.92>1.42
$$

it is worth travelling farther based on this model.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not forget the return journey.
- Compare savings and cost.
- State that the model ignores time, traffic and environmental factors.
- The decision depends on assumptions.

## Concept: Simple Interest Model
Simple interest can be used as a mathematical model for savings with fixed interest rate.

### Formula:
$$
I=Prt
$$

where:
- $I$ = interest.
- $P$ = principal.
- $r$ = interest rate per year.
- $t$ = time in years.

### Example:
Jeremy saves RM$4\,000$ at a simple interest rate of $1.5\%$ per year. He wants to buy a computer worth RM$4\,455$.

The extra amount needed is:

$$
4455-4000=455
$$

So the interest needed is:

$$
I=455
$$

Using:

$$
I=Prt
$$

$$
455=4000(0.015)t
$$

$$
455=60t
$$

$$
t=\frac{455}{60}
$$

$$
t=7.58
$$

Jeremy needs about $7.58$ years.

### Assumptions:
- The interest rate remains constant.
- The computer price does not change.
- Jeremy does not withdraw his savings.
- No additional charges are included.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Convert percentage to decimal.
- $1.5\% = 0.015$.
- Time is measured in years.
- State assumptions when using simple interest.

## Concept: Compound Interest Model
Compound interest is interest calculated on the original principal and accumulated interest.

### Annual Compound Interest Model:
$$
A(t)=P(1+r)^t
$$

where:
- $A(t)$ = amount after $t$ years.
- $P$ = principal.
- $r$ = annual interest rate.
- $t$ = time in years.

### General Compound Interest Model:
If interest is compounded $n$ times per year:

$$
A(t)=P\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}
$$

### Example:
Mr Gomez saves RM$20\,000$ at $4\%$ interest compounded once a year.

$$
A(t)=20000(1+0.04)^t
$$

$$
A(t)=20000(1.04)^t
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Compound interest is exponential.
- The exponent represents the number of compounding periods.
- If compounded monthly, use $n=12$.
- Do not use simple interest formula for compound interest.

## Concept: Exponential Function Model
An exponential model is used when a quantity increases or decreases by a fixed percentage or ratio.

### General Form:
$$
y=Ca^x
$$

where:
- $C$ is the initial value.
- $a$ is a positive constant.
- $a\ne1$.

### Exponential Growth:
If $a>1$, the quantity increases.

Example:

$$
y=2^x
$$

### Exponential Decay:
If $0<a<1$, the quantity decreases.

Example:

$$
y=e^{-x}
$$

### Common Situations:
- Population growth.
- Compound interest.
- Depreciation.
- Cooling patterns.
- Disease spread.
- Weekly increase by percentage.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Exponential change is not the same as adding a fixed amount.
- If the change is by percentage, consider an exponential model.
- Exponential graphs curve sharply.

## Concept: Marathon Training Model
A runner runs $32$ km per week and increases the distance by $10\%$ each week.

### Variables:
- $D(x)$ = distance run after $x$ weeks.
- $x$ = number of weeks.

### Pattern:
Week 1:

$$
32(1.1)
$$

Week 2:

$$
32(1.1)^2
$$

Week 3:

$$
32(1.1)^3
$$

So the model is:

$$
D(x)=32(1.1)^x
$$

### General Model:
If the starting distance is $s$ km and the weekly increase is $r\%$:

$$
D(x)=s\left(1+\frac{r}{100}\right)^x
$$

### Assumptions:
- The runner increases exactly $10\%$ every week.
- The runner does not get injured.
- The routine is followed consistently.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A $10\%$ increase means multiply by $1.10$.
- Do not write $32+0.1x$ because that is linear, not exponential.
- Percentage increase repeatedly usually gives an exponential model.

## Concept: Surface Area to Volume Model
This model can explain why young children may have higher risk of dehydration and hyperthermia in hot weather.

### Key Idea:
- Fluid loss depends on surface area.
- Fluid stored in the body depends on volume.
- A larger surface area-to-volume ratio means faster fluid loss.

### Cube Model:
Use a cube to represent body size.

For a cube with side length $x$:

Surface area:

$$
6x^2
$$

Volume:

$$
x^3
$$

Surface area-to-volume ratio:

$$
y=\frac{6x^2}{x^3}
$$

$$
y=\frac{6}{x}
$$

### Interpretation:
As $x$ increases, the ratio decreases.

This means smaller bodies have a larger surface area-to-volume ratio, so they may lose fluid faster.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- This is a simplified model.
- Human bodies are not cubes.
- The model can be refined using cuboids, cylinders, cones or spheres.
- The model explains the trend, not the exact medical condition.

## Concept: Quadratic Function Model
A quadratic function has a graph in the shape of a parabola.

### General Form:
$$
y=ax^2+bx+c
$$

### When to Use:
Use a quadratic model when:
- The graph rises then falls.
- The graph falls then rises.
- The data forms a curved pattern like a parabola.
- There is a maximum or minimum point.

### Example Situations:
- Cross-section of a river.
- Suspension bridge cable.
- Projectile motion.
- Profit increasing then decreasing after some years.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A quadratic graph is curved, not straight.
- It may have a maximum or minimum point.
- Three points can be used to determine $a$, $b$ and $c$.

## Concept: River Depth Quadratic Model
A hydrologist measures river depth at different distances from the riverbank.

### Given Data:
| Distance from riverbank, $x$ m | Depth, $y$ m |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 |
| 4 | 1.5 |
| 8 | 2.3 |
| 12 | 2.9 |
| 18 | 2.9 |
| 25 | 1.7 |
| 30 | 0 |

### Assumption:
The river is deepest near the middle and has depth $0$ at the edges.

### Model Type:
The data rises and falls like a quadratic graph.

Use:

$$
y=ax^2+bx+c
$$

Using the points:

$$
(0,0),\ (25,1.7),\ (30,0)
$$

Since $(0,0)$ is on the graph:

$$
c=0
$$

Substitute $(25,1.7)$:

$$
1.7=625a+25b
$$

Substitute $(30,0)$:

$$
0=900a+30b
$$

From the equations:

$$
a=-0.0136
$$

$$
b=0.408
$$

So a possible model is:

$$
y=-0.0136x^2+0.408x
$$

When:

$$
x=15
$$

$$
y=-0.0136(15)^2+0.408(15)
$$

$$
y=3.06
$$

So the estimated depth at $15$ m is about $3.06$ m.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The model is approximate.
- More data can make the model more accurate.
- The deepest part may not always be exactly in the middle.
- Explain assumptions clearly.

## Concept: Suspension Bridge Quadratic Model
A suspension bridge cable can be modelled using a quadratic function.

### Assumption:
The lowest point of the cable is at the middle of the bridge.

Let the centre be:

$$
(0,5)
$$

and the two end points be:

$$
(-300,35)
$$

and:

$$
(300,35)
$$

Because the graph is symmetrical:

$$
y=ax^2+c
$$

Since the lowest point is $(0,5)$:

$$
c=5
$$

So:

$$
y=ax^2+5
$$

Use point $(300,35)$:

$$
35=a(300)^2+5
$$

$$
30=90000a
$$

$$
a=\frac{1}{3000}
$$

Therefore, the model is:

$$
y=\frac{1}{3000}x^2+5
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The answer can differ if a different coordinate system is chosen.
- State where the origin is placed.
- Use symmetry to simplify the equation.
- The cable shape is modelled approximately.

## Concept: Altitude and Temperature Linear Model
Temperature usually decreases as altitude increases in the troposphere.

### Given:
- Temperature at sea level is $30^\circ$C.
- Temperature decreases by $6.5^\circ$C per $1000$ m.

### Let:
- $x$ = altitude in metres.
- $T(x)$ = temperature in $^\circ$C.

Rate of decrease per metre:

$$
\frac{6.5}{1000}=0.0065
$$

So the model is:

$$
T(x)=-0.0065x+30
$$

or:

$$
T(x)=-(6.5\times10^{-3})x+30
$$

### Interpretation:
- Gradient $=-0.0065$ means temperature decreases by $0.0065^\circ$C for every $1$ m increase in altitude.
- The $y$-intercept $30$ means the temperature at sea level is $30^\circ$C.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The gradient is negative because temperature decreases.
- Convert $6.5^\circ$C per $1000$ m into per metre.
- The linear assumption is only reasonable within the troposphere and the given context.

## Concept: Choosing a Suitable Function Model
Graphs can help determine the suitable model.

### Linear Model:
Use when the graph resembles a straight line.

$$
y=mx+c
$$

### Quadratic Model:
Use when the graph is a parabola.

$$
y=ax^2+bx+c
$$

### Exponential Model:
Use when the graph increases or decreases sharply by a constant ratio or percentage.

$$
y=Ca^x
$$

### Examples:
| Situation | Suitable Model |
|---|---|
| Petrol used and distance travelled | Linear |
| Temperature decreasing with altitude | Linear |
| River depth cross-section | Quadratic |
| Suspension bridge cable | Quadratic |
| Compound interest | Exponential |
| Population growth or decay | Exponential |
| Weekly distance increases by $10\%$ | Exponential |

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not choose a model only because the formula is familiar.
- Look at the pattern of the data.
- Explain why the model is suitable.
- A model can be valid only within a certain range.

## Concept: Linear, Quadratic and Exponential Graph Patterns

### Linear Graph:
- Straight line.
- Constant rate of change.
- Example: $y=2x+3$.

### Quadratic Graph:
- Parabola.
- Has a maximum or minimum.
- Example: $y=x^2$ or $y=-x^2+4x$.

### Exponential Graph:
- Curved graph with rapid growth or decay.
- Example: $y=2^x$ or $y=e^{-x}$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A curved graph is not automatically quadratic.
- Exponential graphs often increase or decrease very quickly.
- Quadratic graphs usually have turning points.
- Use context and graph shape together.

## Concept: Cooling Model
Cooling data may sometimes be modelled using a linear, quadratic or exponential model.

### Example:
A heated material cools from $200^\circ$C.

| Time (s) | Temperature ($^\circ$C) |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 200 |
| 40 | 140 |
| 80 | 100 |
| 120 | 75 |

### Interpretation:
- A linear model may become unrealistic if it predicts temperature below $0^\circ$C too soon.
- A quadratic model may become unrealistic if it later predicts temperature increasing again.
- An exponential decay model may be more suitable because cooling often slows down over time.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Choose the model that makes sense over the required time interval.
- A model may fit early data but fail later.
- Explain why rejected models are not suitable.

## Concept: Model Validity
A model is valid only when it gives reasonable results within the intended context.

### Things to Check:
- Is the result possible?
- Is the result within the real-world range?
- Does the model work outside the original data range?
- Are the assumptions still true?
- Does the model behave logically?

### Example:
A linear temperature model may be valid from $0$ m to $4000$ m altitude, but not necessarily for all altitudes.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not extend a model too far beyond the given data.
- If a model gives impossible values, explain its limitation.
- Validity depends on the context.

## Concept: Model Limitations
A limitation is something the model does not include or cannot explain.

### Examples:
The rice-store model may ignore:
- Traffic jam.
- Time taken.
- Parking cost.
- Toll.
- Road distance.
- Environmental cost.
- Changes in petrol price.

The river-depth model may ignore:
- River current.
- Uneven riverbed.
- Measurement error.
- Seasonal water level change.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Every model has limitations.
- Limitations should be stated honestly.
- Acknowledging limitations improves the quality of the answer.

## Concept: Refining a Model with More Data
A model can be improved by collecting more data or using better assumptions.

### Examples:
- Use real road distance instead of straight-line distance.
- Use different petrol consumption rates for highway and city driving.
- Use more river-depth measurements.
- Use monthly compounding instead of annual compounding.
- Include time cost and toll cost in a travel-cost model.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- More realistic models may be more complicated.
- More data can improve accuracy.
- The best model balances simplicity and usefulness.

## Concept: Reporting a Mathematical Modeling Solution
A complete modeling answer should follow a clear structure.

### Suggested Structure:
1. **Problem:** State what needs to be solved.
2. **Assumptions:** State simplifications.
3. **Variables:** Define symbols and units.
4. **Model:** Write equations or describe graph.
5. **Solution:** Show calculations.
6. **Verification:** Check reasonableness.
7. **Refinement:** Suggest improvements.
8. **Conclusion:** Answer the real-world question.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Use proper mathematical notation.
- Use units in the final answer.
- Write explanations in context.
- Do not simply copy a previous example as a template; each modeling problem may need a different model.

S=(P_1-P_2)n
$$

$$
T=\frac{s}{m}\times H \times 2
$$

If:

$$
S>T
$$

then travelling farther is worthwhile based on the model.

## Common Mistakes & Exam Tips for the Whole Chapter
- State assumptions before forming a model.
- Define all variables clearly.
- Include units.
- Choose the model based on graph pattern and context.
- Linear means constant rate.
- Quadratic means parabolic pattern.
- Exponential often means repeated percentage change.
- Check whether the answer is realistic.
- Explain limitations of the model.
- Refine the model if assumptions are too simple.
- In reporting, include both mathematical working and real-world interpretation.
- Open-ended modeling problems can have more than one valid answer.
