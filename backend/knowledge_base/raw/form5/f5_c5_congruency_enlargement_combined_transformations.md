# Topic: Congruency, Enlargement and Combined Transformations
**Form:** Form 5 | **Chapter:** Chapter 5 | **Curriculum:** SPM KSSM

## Overview
This chapter covers congruent shapes, triangle congruency, similar objects, enlargement, scale factor, area change under enlargement, combined transformations, commutative law in transformations, and tessellation. Students learn how to compare shapes using sides and angles, describe enlargement using centre and scale factor, determine images and objects under combined transformations, and identify or design tessellations.

## Concept: Congruency
Two figures or objects are congruent if they have the same shape and the same size, even if their position, direction, or arrangement is different.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Congruent shapes have equal corresponding sides.
- Congruent shapes have equal corresponding angles.
- The shape may be rotated, reflected, or translated, but its size and shape remain unchanged.
- If one corresponding side or angle is not equal, the shapes are not congruent.

### Notation:
If triangle $ABC$ is congruent to triangle $PQR$, it can be written as:

$$
\triangle ABC \cong \triangle PQR
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Same shape but different size means similar, not congruent.
- Same size but different shape is not congruent.
- Always compare corresponding sides and corresponding angles.
- Do not compare random sides or angles.

## Concept: Corresponding Sides and Corresponding Angles
Corresponding sides and angles are the matching sides and angles between two shapes.

### Example:
If quadrilateral $ABCD$ is congruent to quadrilateral $PQRS$, then possible corresponding sides are:

$$
AB = RQ
$$

$$
BC = QP
$$

$$
CD = PS
$$

$$
AD = RS
$$

and corresponding angles are:

$$
\angle BAD = \angle QRS
$$

$$
\angle ABC = \angle RQP
$$

$$
\angle BCD = \angle QPS
$$

$$
\angle ADC = \angle RSP
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Match vertices in the correct order.
- Use the diagram carefully because the shape may be turned or reflected.
- Congruency depends on both side lengths and angle sizes.

## Concept: Determining Whether Shapes Are Congruent
To determine whether two shapes are congruent, compare all corresponding sides and angles.

### Method:
1. Identify the matching sides.
2. Identify the matching angles.
3. Check whether all corresponding sides are equal.
4. Check whether all corresponding angles are equal.
5. Conclude whether the shapes are congruent.

### Worked Example:
Given two right triangles.

First triangle has sides $5$ cm and $12$ cm, so the hypotenuse is:

$$
\sqrt{5^2 + 12^2}
$$

$$
= \sqrt{25 + 144}
$$

$$
= \sqrt{169}
$$

$$
= 13
$$

If the second triangle has matching side lengths $5$ cm, $12$ cm and $13$ cm, and the angles also match, then the two triangles are congruent.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- If only one side is different, the shapes are not congruent.
- If only one angle is different, the shapes are not congruent.
- A shape can still be congruent even if it faces a different direction.

## Concept: Triangle Congruency
Two triangles are congruent if they have enough equal corresponding sides and angles to prove that they are exactly the same shape and size.

**Main Triangle Congruency Rules:**
- Side-Side-Side (SSS)
- Side-Angle-Side (SAS)
- Angle-Side-Angle (ASA)
- Angle-Angle-Side (AAS)
- Angle-Angle-Angle (AAA), only with equal area
- Side-Side-Angle (SSA), only with equal area
- Right angle-Hypotenuse-Side (RHS), for right-angled triangles

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- AAA alone usually proves similarity, not congruency, unless the areas are equal.
- SSA alone is not always enough unless the areas are equal.
- SAS must use the angle between the two given sides.

## Concept: Side-Side-Side Congruency
Side-Side-Side, or SSS, means all three corresponding sides of two triangles are equal.

### Key Rule:
If:

$$
AB = PQ
$$

$$
BC = QR
$$

$$
AC = PR
$$

then:

$$
\triangle ABC \cong \triangle PQR
$$

by SSS.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- SSS compares three sides only.
- No angle information is needed if all three corresponding sides are equal.
- Make sure the sides are corresponding sides.

## Concept: Side-Angle-Side Congruency
Side-Angle-Side, or SAS, means two corresponding sides and the included angle between them are equal.

### Key Rule:
If:

$$
AB = PQ
$$

$$
\angle BAC = \angle QPR
$$

$$
AC = PR
$$

then:

$$
\triangle ABC \cong \triangle PQR
$$

by SAS.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The angle must be between the two given sides.
- If the angle is not between the two given sides, it is SSA, not SAS.
- SAS is commonly used in proof questions.

## Concept: Angle-Side-Angle Congruency
Angle-Side-Angle, or ASA, means two corresponding angles and the side between them are equal.

### Key Rule:
If:

$$
\angle BAC = \angle QPR
$$

$$
AC = PR
$$

$$
\angle ACB = \angle PRQ
$$

then:

$$
\triangle ABC \cong \triangle PQR
$$

by ASA.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The side must lie between the two given angles.
- If the side is not between the two angles, the rule is AAS.
- ASA is useful when diagrams show parallel lines and equal angles.

## Concept: Angle-Angle-Side Congruency
Angle-Angle-Side, or AAS, means two corresponding angles and one corresponding side not between the two angles are equal.

### Key Rule:
If:

$$
\angle BAC = \angle QPR
$$

$$
\angle ACB = \angle PRQ
$$

$$
BC = QR
$$

then:

$$
\triangle ABC \cong \triangle PQR
$$

by AAS.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- AAS has two angles and one side.
- The given side is not between the two angles.
- AAS can prove congruency because the third angle is automatically fixed.

## Concept: Angle-Angle-Angle and Side-Side-Angle
AAA and SSA need extra care.

### AAA:
AAA means all three corresponding angles are equal.

However:
- AAA alone usually shows similarity.
- To prove congruency using AAA, the areas of the two triangles must also be equal.

### SSA:
SSA means two corresponding sides and one corresponding angle not between the two sides are equal.

However:
- SSA alone may not always prove congruency.
- To prove congruency using SSA, the areas of the two triangles must also be equal.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not use AAA alone to prove congruency unless equal area is given.
- Do not use SSA alone unless equal area is given.
- In most exam questions, SSS, SAS, ASA and AAS are safer rules.

## Concept: Right Angle-Hypotenuse-Side
Right angle-Hypotenuse-Side, or RHS, is a special congruency rule for right-angled triangles.

### Key Rule:
Two right-angled triangles are congruent if:
- Both have a right angle.
- Their hypotenuse lengths are equal.
- One corresponding non-hypotenuse side is equal.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- RHS only applies to right-angled triangles.
- The hypotenuse is the longest side opposite the right angle.
- Do not use RHS for triangles without right angles.

## Concept: Solving Congruency Proof Problems
Congruency proof questions usually ask students to show that two triangles are congruent.

### General Method:
1. Identify the two triangles.
2. Find equal sides from the diagram or given information.
3. Find equal angles from the diagram or geometric rules.
4. Choose the correct congruency rule.
5. Write the conclusion.

### Worked Example:
In an isosceles triangle $PQR$, it is given that:

$$
PR = QR
$$

$A$ and $B$ are midpoints of $PR$ and $QR$ respectively.

Then:

$$
BR = AR
$$

Also:

$$
\angle PRB = \angle QRA
$$

because both angles are formed at the same vertex region.

So triangles $PBR$ and $QAR$ satisfy SAS.

Therefore:

$$
\triangle PBR \cong \triangle QAR
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- State the congruency rule clearly.
- Do not just say “they look the same”.
- Use midpoint, parallel lines, vertical opposite angles, or equal sides when provided.

## Concept: Similarity
Two geometric objects are similar if they have the same shape but may have different sizes.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
Two objects are similar when:
- All corresponding angles are equal.
- All ratios of corresponding sides are equal.

### For Triangles:
If all corresponding angles are equal, then the triangles are similar.

If all corresponding sides are proportional, then the triangles are also similar.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Similar shapes do not need to have the same size.
- Congruent shapes are always similar, but similar shapes are not always congruent.
- If corresponding side ratios are not equal, the shapes are not similar.

## Concept: Determining Whether Shapes Are Similar
To determine whether two shapes are similar, compare their corresponding angles and side ratios.

### Method:
1. Check whether corresponding angles are equal.
2. Check whether corresponding side ratios are equal.
3. If both are true, the shapes are similar.

### Worked Example:
If two triangles have angles:

$$
47^\circ,\quad 79^\circ,\quad 54^\circ
$$

and the other triangle also has:

$$
47^\circ,\quad 79^\circ,\quad 54^\circ
$$

then the triangles are similar because all corresponding angles are equal.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Equal angles alone are enough for triangle similarity.
- For quadrilaterals or polygons, also check side ratios.
- Do not assume two shapes are similar only because they look alike.

## Concept: Enlargement
Enlargement is a transformation where all points of an object move from a fixed point with a constant ratio.

**Key Terms:**
- The fixed point is called the centre of enlargement.
- The constant ratio is called the scale factor.
- The object and its image under enlargement are similar.

### Scale Factor Formula:
$$
k =
\frac{\text{distance of point of image from centre}}{\text{distance of point of object from centre}}
$$

or:

$$
k =
\frac{\text{length of side of image}}{\text{length of side of object}}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Enlargement changes size but keeps shape similar.
- The centre of enlargement must be stated when describing an enlargement.
- The scale factor must also be stated.

## Concept: Effects of Scale Factor
The scale factor determines the size and position of the image.

| Scale Factor | Size of Image | Position of Image |
|---|---|---|
| $k > 1$ | Larger than object | Same side as object |
| $k = 1$ | Same size as object | Same side as object |
| $0 < k < 1$ | Smaller than object | Same side as object |
| $-1 < k < 0$ | Smaller than object | Opposite side of centre |
| $k = -1$ | Same size as object | Opposite side of centre |
| $k < -1$ | Larger than object | Opposite side of centre |

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Positive scale factor means image is on the same side of the centre as the object.
- Negative scale factor means image is on the opposite side of the centre.
- A negative scale factor also causes the image to appear inverted.
- $k=1$ does not change the size.
- $k=-1$ gives the same size but on the opposite side.

## Concept: Describing an Enlargement
To describe an enlargement, state:
1. The centre of enlargement.
2. The scale factor.

### Example Statement:
Triangle $A'B'C'$ is the image of triangle $ABC$ under an enlargement at centre $(-2,-1)$ with scale factor:

$$
k = \frac{3}{2}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not write only the scale factor.
- Do not write only the centre.
- A complete description needs both centre and scale factor.

## Concept: Finding the Centre of Enlargement
The centre of enlargement can be found by drawing straight lines through each pair of corresponding points.

### Method:
1. Join each object point to its corresponding image point.
2. Extend the lines if needed.
3. The intersection point of the lines is the centre of enlargement.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Use at least two pairs of corresponding points.
- The centre may be outside the object and image.
- For negative scale factors, the centre lies between the object and image.

## Concept: Determining Object and Image in Enlargement
Given two similar figures and a scale factor, identify which one is the object and which one is the image.

### Key Rules:
- If $k>1$, the image is larger than the object.
- If $0<k<1$, the image is smaller than the object.
- If $k<0$, the image is on the opposite side of the centre from the object.
- If $|k|>1$, the image is larger.
- If $|k|<1$, the image is smaller.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not use the larger figure as the image automatically; check the scale factor.
- For negative scale factor, consider both size and opposite direction.
- Object-to-image distance follows the scale factor.

## Concept: Drawing an Image Under Enlargement
To draw an image under enlargement:

1. Identify the centre of enlargement.
2. Identify the scale factor.
3. Draw projection lines from the centre to each vertex.
4. Multiply each distance from the centre by the scale factor.
5. Mark the image points.
6. Join the image points.

### For Positive Scale Factor:
The image is on the same side of the centre as the object.

### For Negative Scale Factor:
The image is on the opposite side of the centre from the object.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Measure from the centre of enlargement, not from the origin.
- For negative scale factor, go in the opposite direction.
- Keep the image similar to the object.

## Concept: Drawing the Object When Image Is Given
Sometimes the image is given and the object must be found.

### Method:
Use the reciprocal of the scale factor.

If the image was produced using:

$$
k = 2
$$

then to go from image back to object, use:

$$
\frac{1}{2}
$$

If:

$$
k = -3
$$

then to go from image back to object, use:

$$
-\frac{1}{3}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- To reverse an enlargement, use the reciprocal scale factor.
- Keep the sign of the scale factor.
- If the original scale factor is negative, the reverse scale factor is also negative.

## Concept: Area of Image Under Enlargement
Under enlargement, area changes according to the square of the scale factor.

### Formula:
$$
\frac{\text{Area of image}}{\text{Area of object}} = k^2
$$

Therefore:

$$
\text{Area of image} = k^2 \times \text{Area of object}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Area uses $k^2$, not $k$.
- A negative scale factor still gives a positive area ratio because $k^2$ is positive.
- Length changes by $k$, but area changes by $k^2$.

## Concept: Finding Scale Factor from Area
If the area of object and image are given:

$$
k^2 =
\frac{\text{Area of image}}{\text{Area of object}}
$$

Then:

$$
k = \pm \sqrt{\frac{\text{Area of image}}{\text{Area of object}}}
$$

The sign depends on the position of the image:
- Same side of centre: positive.
- Opposite side of centre: negative.

### Worked Example:
Area of object:

$$
5\text{ cm}^2
$$

Area of image:

$$
45\text{ cm}^2
$$

Then:

$$
k^2 = \frac{45}{5}
$$

$$
k^2 = 9
$$

$$
k = 3 \text{ or } -3
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not forget the square root.
- If position is not given, both positive and negative scale factors may be possible.
- If the image is on the opposite side of the centre, the scale factor is negative.

## Concept: Solving Enlargement Problems
Enlargement word problems often involve length, area, scale factor, and shaded regions.

### General Method:
1. Identify object and image.
2. Find the scale factor using side lengths or distances.
3. Use $k$ for length.
4. Use $k^2$ for area.
5. Subtract areas if a shaded region is involved.

### Worked Example:
Trapezium $AEFG$ is the image of trapezium $ABCD$ under enlargement at centre $A$.

If:

$$
AB = 20
$$

and:

$$
BE = 16
$$

then:

$$
AE = 20 + 16 = 36
$$

Scale factor:

$$
k = \frac{AE}{AB}
$$

$$
= \frac{36}{20}
$$

$$
= \frac{9}{5}
$$

If the coloured region is $168\text{ cm}^2$, then use:

$$
\text{Area of image} = k^2 \times \text{Area of object}
$$

and subtract the object area from the image area.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- If a side is split into two parts, add the parts first.
- Use $k^2$ for area.
- For shaded regions, identify whether it is image area minus object area.

## Concept: Combined Transformation
A combined transformation means applying more than one transformation to an object.

The transformations in this chapter include:
- Translation.
- Reflection.
- Rotation.
- Enlargement.

### Key Rule:
Combined transformation $AB$ means transformation $B$ is performed first, followed by transformation $A$.

So:

$$
AB = B \text{ followed by } A
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The order is very important.
- $AB$ does not mean $A$ followed by $B$.
- Always read combined transformations from right to left.

## Concept: Determining Image Under Combined Transformation
To determine the image of an object under combined transformation $AB$:

1. Perform transformation $B$ first.
2. Obtain the first image.
3. Perform transformation $A$ on the first image.
4. The result is the final image.

### Example:
If:

$$
P = \text{translation } \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

then $P^2$ means transformation $P$ is performed two times.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- For $P^2$, apply $P$ twice.
- For $RQ$, apply $Q$ first, then $R$.
- Label the first image to avoid confusion.

## Concept: Determining Object When Image Is Given
To determine the object when the image is given, reverse the transformations.

### Reverse Rules:
| Transformation | Reverse Transformation |
|---|---|
| Translation $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}$ | Translation $\begin{pmatrix} -a \\ -b \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Reflection | Same reflection |
| Clockwise rotation | Anticlockwise rotation |
| Anticlockwise rotation | Clockwise rotation |
| Enlargement with scale factor $k$ | Enlargement with scale factor $\frac{1}{k}$ |

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Reverse the order when working from image back to object.
- Reflection is its own inverse.
- For enlargement, use reciprocal scale factor.
- For rotation, reverse the direction.

## Concept: Commutative Law in Combined Transformation
A combined transformation satisfies the commutative law if changing the order gives the same final image.

### Meaning:
Transformation $AB$ satisfies the commutative law if:

$$
AB = BA
$$

This means the image produced by $AB$ is the same as the image produced by $BA$.

If the images are different, then:

$$
AB \ne BA
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Most combined transformations do not satisfy the commutative law.
- Do not assume $AB = BA$.
- Check by applying both orders and comparing the final images.

## Concept: Describing a Combined Transformation
To describe a combined transformation, identify the transformations that connect the object to the final image.

### Key Points:
- Congruent image with same orientation may indicate translation.
- Congruent image with inverted orientation may indicate reflection or rotation.
- Similar but different size indicates enlargement.
- Different distances between corresponding points may indicate enlargement.

### For Combined Transformation $AB$:
Describe transformation $B$ first, then transformation $A$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- If the image size changes, enlargement is involved.
- If the image is congruent but reversed, reflection may be involved.
- If the image turns around a point, rotation may be involved.
- If all points move the same distance and direction, translation is involved.

## Concept: Equivalent Single Transformation
Sometimes a combined transformation can be described as one single transformation.

### Example:
A rotation followed by enlargement may sometimes be equivalent to one enlargement with a negative scale factor.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Not all combined transformations can be described by one single transformation.
- If a single transformation is required, compare object and final image directly.
- State the centre and scale factor for enlargement.
- State the centre and angle for rotation.
- State the mirror line for reflection.

## Concept: Area Under Combined Transformation
If the combined transformation includes enlargement, the area changes by $k^2$.

### Formula:
$$
\text{Area of image} = k^2 \times \text{Area of object}
$$

If the transformation is only translation, reflection, or rotation, then:

$$
\text{Area of image} = \text{Area of object}
$$

because these are isometric transformations.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Translation, reflection and rotation do not change area.
- Enlargement changes area.
- Use $k^2$, not $k$, when calculating area after enlargement.

## Concept: Tessellation
A tessellation is a pattern of recurring shapes that fills a plane without leaving gaps or overlapping.

### Examples:
- Tessellation using equilateral triangles.
- Tessellation using rhombuses.
- Tessellation using squares and regular octagons.
- Tessellation using repeated pentagon patterns.
- Tessellation using repeating carpet-like patterns.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A pattern with gaps is not a tessellation.
- A pattern with overlapping shapes is not a tessellation.
- A pattern with a non-recurring shape may not be considered a tessellation.
- The shapes must cover the plane continuously.

## Concept: Identifying Tessellations
To determine whether a pattern is a tessellation:

1. Check whether the pattern repeats.
2. Check whether the shapes fill the plane.
3. Check whether there are no gaps.
4. Check whether there are no overlaps.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A nice-looking pattern is not always a tessellation.
- The key condition is no gaps and no overlaps.
- Repetition is important.

## Concept: Designing Tessellations
Tessellations can be designed using isometric transformations.

### Isometric Transformations:
Isometric transformations produce images congruent to the object.

They include:
- Translation.
- Reflection.
- Rotation.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Enlargement is not an isometric transformation because it changes size.
- Tessellation usually uses congruent repeated shapes.
- Translation is commonly used to repeat a shape across a plane.

## Concept: Tessellation Using Translation
A shape can be repeated by sliding it in a fixed direction.

### Translation Form:
A translation can be written as:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
a \\
b
\end{pmatrix}
$$

where:
- $a$ is horizontal movement.
- $b$ is vertical movement.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Translation does not change size, shape, or orientation.
- The image is congruent to the original shape.
- Translation is useful for repeating rows or columns of tessellation.

## Concept: Tessellation Using Reflection
A shape can be reflected across a line to create a repeating pattern.

### Common Mirror Lines:
- Vertical lines.
- Horizontal lines.
- Slanted lines.
- Sides of polygons.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Reflection reverses orientation.
- The image remains congruent to the object.
- The mirror line must be stated clearly.

## Concept: Tessellation Using Rotation
A shape can be rotated around a point to form repeated patterns.

### Common Rotation Angles:
- $60^\circ$
- $90^\circ$
- $120^\circ$
- $180^\circ$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- State the angle and direction of rotation.
- State the centre of rotation.
- Rotated images are congruent to the original shape.

## Concept: Escher Tessellation
An Escher tessellation is a tessellation where the repeated shapes are designed to look like real-life objects or recognisable figures.

### Key Idea:
- A normal tessellation may use simple polygons.
- An Escher tessellation modifies shapes creatively while still filling the plane without gaps or overlaps.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The pattern must still repeat without gaps or overlaps.
- The design may look artistic, but it must follow tessellation rules.
- Transformations such as translation, reflection and rotation can be used.

## Concept: Summary of Key Formulas and Rules

### Congruent Shapes
Congruent shapes have equal corresponding sides and angles.

$$
\text{Same shape} + \text{same size} = \text{congruent}
$$

### Similar Shapes
Similar shapes have equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding sides.

$$
\frac{\text{Side of image}}{\text{Corresponding side of object}}
=
\text{constant ratio}
$$

### Scale Factor
$$
k =
\frac{\text{distance of image point from centre}}{\text{distance of object point from centre}}
$$

or:

$$
k =
\frac{\text{length of side of image}}{\text{length of side of object}}
$$

### Area Under Enlargement
$$
\frac{\text{Area of image}}{\text{Area of object}} = k^2
$$

$$
\text{Area of image} = k^2 \times \text{Area of object}
$$

### Combined Transformation
$$
AB = B \text{ followed by } A
$$

### Reverse Translation
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
a \\
b
\end{pmatrix}
\rightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}
-a \\
-b
\end{pmatrix}
$$

### Reverse Enlargement
$$
k \rightarrow \frac{1}{k}
$$

### Tessellation
A tessellation is a recurring pattern that fills a plane:

$$
\text{No gaps} + \text{no overlaps} + \text{repeating shapes}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Congruency requires same shape and same size.
- Similarity requires same shape, but size may differ.
- Enlargement produces similar objects.
- Area changes by $k^2$, not $k$.
- Combined transformation $AB$ means $B$ first, then $A$.
- Reflection, rotation and translation are isometric transformations.
- Tessellation must have no gaps and no overlaps.
