# Topic: Quadratic Functions and Equations in One Variable
**Form:** Form 4 | **Chapter:** Chapter 1 | **Curriculum:** SPM KSSM

## Overview
Quadratic functions and equations are used to model situations involving curved shapes, maximum or minimum values, and unknown quantities. In this topic, students learn how to identify quadratic expressions, understand quadratic graphs, form quadratic equations from situations, and determine roots using factorisation or graphical methods.

## Concept: Quadratic Expression in One Variable
A quadratic expression in one variable is an algebraic expression where the highest power of the variable is $2$.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- The general form of a quadratic expression is:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c
$$

- $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants.
- $a \neq 0$.
- $x$ is the variable.
- $a$ is the coefficient of $x^2$.
- $b$ is the coefficient of $x$.
- $c$ is the constant.
- Other letters besides $x$ can also be used as variables.
- The values of $b$ and $c$ may be $0$, but $a$ cannot be $0$.
- Examples of quadratic expressions include $x^2 + 2x - 1$, $-y^2 + 3y$, $\frac{1}{3}m^2 - m + 4$, and $2n^2 + 5$.

### Worked Example:
Determine whether each expression is a quadratic expression in one variable.

(a) $2x^2 + 5$

This is a quadratic expression in one variable because the highest power of $x$ is $2$.

(b) $x^3 - 6$

This is not a quadratic expression because the highest power of $x$ is $3$.

(c) $3x^2 + 2y + 1$

This is not a quadratic expression in one variable because it contains two variables, $x$ and $y$.

(d) $\frac{1}{2}m^2$

This is a quadratic expression in one variable because the highest power of $m$ is $2$.

(e) $2x^2 - \frac{3}{x^2}$

This is not a quadratic expression because it contains a variable with a power that is not a whole number.

(f) $4x^2 - x^{\frac{1}{2}}$

This is not a quadratic expression because $x^{\frac{1}{2}}$ is not a whole-number power.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not classify an expression as quadratic if the highest power is not $2$.
- Do not classify an expression as a quadratic expression in one variable if it contains more than one variable.
- Expressions involving powers such as $x^{-2}$ or $x^{\frac{1}{2}}$ are not quadratic expressions.

## Concept: Quadratic Function
A quadratic function is written in function notation and has the general form:

$$
f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c
$$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants and $a \neq 0$.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- A quadratic expression is written as $ax^2 + bx + c$.
- A quadratic function is written as $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$.
- For a quadratic function, $y = f(x)$.
- The graph of a quadratic function is called a parabola.
- A quadratic function is a many-to-one relation because two different values of $x$ can have the same image value $f(x)$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not confuse a quadratic expression with a quadratic function.
- A quadratic function must still have $a \neq 0$.

## Concept: Shape of the Graph of a Quadratic Function
The graph of a quadratic function has only two possible shapes depending on the value of $a$.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$:
  - If $a > 0$, the parabola opens upwards.
  - If $a < 0$, the parabola opens downwards.
- The value of $a$ determines the shape of the graph.
- The smaller the value of $a$, the wider the curve of the graph.
- The larger the value of $a$, the narrower the curve of the graph.

**Visual Meaning:**
- If $a > 0$, the graph is a U-shaped parabola opening upwards.
- If $a < 0$, the graph is an inverted U-shaped parabola opening downwards.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A positive value of $a$ gives a minimum point.
- A negative value of $a$ gives a maximum point.
- The sign of $a$ affects the direction of the graph, not the value of the $y$-intercept.

## Concept: Maximum and Minimum Points
The graph of a quadratic function has either a maximum point or a minimum point depending on the value of $a$.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$:
  - If $a < 0$, the graph has a maximum point.
  - If $a > 0$, the graph has a minimum point.
- The maximum point is the highest point on the parabola.
- The minimum point is the lowest point on the parabola.
- The maximum or minimum point is also called a stationary point or turning point.

**Visual Meaning:**
- A downward-opening parabola has a highest point.
- An upward-opening parabola has a lowest point.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not state that every quadratic graph has a maximum point.
- Check the sign of $a$ first before deciding whether the graph has a maximum or minimum point.

## Concept: Axis of Symmetry
The axis of symmetry of a quadratic graph is a vertical straight line that divides the graph into two equal parts.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- The axis of symmetry is parallel to the $y$-axis.
- The axis of symmetry passes through the maximum or minimum point.
- Each quadratic graph has one axis of symmetry.
- If the turning point is $(h, k)$, the axis of symmetry is:

$$
x = h
$$

- If the turning point is $(m, n)$, the axis of symmetry is:

$$
x = m
$$

- The equation of the axis of symmetry for $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ is:

$$
x = -\frac{b}{2a}
$$

- If $b = 0$, the $y$-axis is the axis of symmetry.

**Visual Meaning:**
- The axis of symmetry is a vertical dashed line through the centre of the parabola.
- It passes through the highest point for a graph with $a < 0$.
- It passes through the lowest point for a graph with $a > 0$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The axis of symmetry is written as $x = h$, not $y = h$.
- The axis of symmetry is parallel to the $y$-axis, not the $x$-axis.

## Concept: Effects of Changing the Values of $a$, $b$, and $c$
In the quadratic function $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$, the values of $a$, $b$, and $c$ affect different features of the graph.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- The value of $a$ determines the shape and direction of the graph.
- The value of $b$ determines the position of the axis of symmetry.
- The value of $c$ determines the position of the $y$-intercept.

For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$:

If $a > 0$:
- When $b > 0$, the axis of symmetry lies on the left of the $y$-axis.
- When $b < 0$, the axis of symmetry lies on the right of the $y$-axis.
- When $b = 0$, the axis of symmetry is the $y$-axis.

If $a < 0$:
- When $b > 0$, the axis of symmetry lies on the right of the $y$-axis.
- When $b < 0$, the axis of symmetry lies on the left of the $y$-axis.
- When $b = 0$, the axis of symmetry is the $y$-axis.

For the $y$-intercept:
- The value of $c$ is the $y$-intercept.
- The graph intersects the $y$-axis at $c$.

### Worked Example:
The quadratic function $f(x) = x^2 - 3x + c$ passes through point $A(0, 4)$. Find $c$.

Since $A(0, 4)$ lies on the $y$-axis, the $y$-intercept is $4$.

Therefore:

$$
c = 4
$$

The same function passes through point $A(-1, 3)$. Find $c$.

Substitute $x = -1$ and $f(x) = 3$:

$$
3 = (-1)^2 - 3(-1) + c
$$

$$
3 = 1 + 3 + c
$$

$$
c = -1
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- If a point has $x = 0$, its $y$-coordinate is the $y$-intercept.
- Do not assume $c$ from the graph shape; $c$ depends on where the graph crosses the $y$-axis.

## Concept: Forming Quadratic Functions and Equations from Situations
Quadratic functions and equations can be formed from real-life situations involving area, age, distance, or other quantities.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- A quadratic function is written as:

$$
f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c
$$

- A quadratic equation is written in the general form:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = 0
$$

- To form a quadratic equation:
  - Define the unknown variable.
  - Form an expression from the situation.
  - Set the expression equal to the given value.
  - Rearrange the equation into the form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

### Worked Example:
Mr Ganesan makes two cards for Mathematics Club activities.

One card is a square with side $x$ cm.

The other card is a rectangle with length $(2x + 1)$ cm and width $x$ cm.

(a) Form a quadratic expression for the total area, $A$ cm$^2$, in terms of $x$.

The total area is:

$$
A = x^2 + x(2x + 1)
$$

$$
A = x^2 + 2x^2 + x
$$

$$
A = 3x^2 + x
$$

(b) If the total area is $114$ cm$^2$, form a quadratic equation in terms of $x$.

$$
3x^2 + x = 114
$$

$$
3x^2 + x - 114 = 0
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Always rearrange a quadratic equation into $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ when required.
- For area problems, multiply length by width carefully before simplifying.
- Do not forget units such as cm$^2$ or m$^2$ when interpreting area.

## Concept: Roots of a Quadratic Equation
The roots of a quadratic equation are the values of the variable that satisfy the equation.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- The general form of a quadratic equation is:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = 0
$$

- The roots are the values of $x$ that make the equation true.
- A quadratic equation may have one or two roots.
- A value satisfies an equation if substituting the value makes the left side equal to the right side.

### Worked Example:
Determine whether $x = 1$ and $x = 3$ are roots of:

$$
2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0
$$

When $x = 1$:

$$
2(1)^2 - 7(1) + 3 = 2 - 7 + 3
$$

$$
= -2
$$

Since $-2 \neq 0$, $x = 1$ is not a root.

When $x = 3$:

$$
2(3)^2 - 7(3) + 3 = 18 - 21 + 3
$$

$$
= 0
$$

Since the value is $0$, $x = 3$ is a root.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A root must make the equation true.
- For equations not written as $= 0$, compare the left side and right side after substitution.
- Do not assume a value is a root just because it appears in the question.

## Concept: Relationship Between Roots and $x$-Intercepts
The roots of a quadratic equation are related to the points where the quadratic graph intersects the $x$-axis.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- The roots of:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = 0
$$

are the $x$-values where the graph of:

$$
f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c
$$

intersects the $x$-axis.
- These points are also called $x$-intercepts.
- At the $x$-axis, the value of $f(x)$ is $0$.

**Visual Meaning:**
- If the graph crosses the $x$-axis at $x = -4$ and $x = 1.5$, the roots are $-4$ and $1.5$.
- If the graph crosses the $x$-axis at $x = -1$ and $x = 4$, the roots are $-1$ and $4$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- The roots are the $x$-coordinates of the intercepts, not the full coordinates.
- The graph may intersect the $x$-axis at two points or touch it at one point.

## Concept: Solving Quadratic Equations by Factorisation
Factorisation is one method used to determine the roots of a quadratic equation.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Before factorising, write the quadratic equation in the form:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = 0
$$

- Factorise the quadratic expression.
- Set each factor equal to $0$.
- Solve for the variable.

### Worked Example:
Determine the roots of:

$$
x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0
$$

Factorise:

$$
(x - 3)(x - 2) = 0
$$

Set each factor equal to $0$:

$$
x - 3 = 0
$$

$$
x = 3
$$

or

$$
x - 2 = 0
$$

$$
x = 2
$$

Therefore, the roots are $x = 3$ and $x = 2$.

### Worked Example:
Determine the roots of:

$$
x^2 + \frac{7}{2}x = 2
$$

Multiply by $2$:

$$
2x^2 + 7x = 4
$$

Rearrange into general form:

$$
2x^2 + 7x - 4 = 0
$$

Factorise:

$$
(2x - 1)(x + 4) = 0
$$

Therefore:

$$
x = \frac{1}{2}
$$

or

$$
x = -4
$$

### Worked Example:
Determine the roots of:

$$
(y + 2)(y + 1) = 2(y + 11)
$$

Expand both sides:

$$
y^2 + 3y + 2 = 2y + 22
$$

Rearrange:

$$
y^2 + y - 20 = 0
$$

Factorise:

$$
(y + 5)(y - 4) = 0
$$

Therefore:

$$
y = -5
$$

or

$$
y = 4
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Always rearrange the equation into $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ before factorising.
- If the equation contains fractions, clear the fractions first.
- If the equation contains brackets, expand and simplify before factorising.
- If a denominator contains the variable, multiply carefully and consider restrictions from the denominator.

## Concept: Solving Quadratic Equations by Graphical Method
The roots of a quadratic equation can also be determined using the graph of the corresponding quadratic function.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- To solve:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = 0
$$

draw or observe the graph:

$$
f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c
$$

- Read the values of $x$ where the graph intersects the $x$-axis.
- These $x$-values are the roots.

**Visual Meaning:**
- The graph of $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ intersects the $x$-axis where $f(x) = 0$.
- The intersection points represent the roots of the equation.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Read the $x$-values, not the $y$-values, when identifying roots from a graph.
- If the graph touches the $x$-axis at one point, the equation has one repeated root.
- If the graph does not intersect the $x$-axis, there are no real roots shown on the graph.

## Concept: Sketching Graphs of Quadratic Functions
A sketch of a quadratic function should show its important features clearly.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
When sketching the graph of a quadratic function, show:
- The correct shape of the graph.
- The $y$-intercept.
- The $x$-intercepts or at least one point that passes through the graph.

For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$:
- Use the sign of $a$ to determine whether the graph opens upwards or downwards.
- Use $c$ to identify the $y$-intercept.
- Solve $f(x) = 0$ to find the $x$-intercepts.

### Worked Example:
Sketch the graph of:

$$
f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3
$$

Identify the values:

$$
a = 1,\quad b = -4,\quad c = 3
$$

Since $a = 1 > 0$, the graph opens upwards.

Since $c = 3$, the $y$-intercept is $3$.

Find the $x$-intercepts:

$$
x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0
$$

$$
(x - 3)(x - 1) = 0
$$

$$
x = 3
$$

or

$$
x = 1
$$

The graph opens upwards, crosses the $y$-axis at $3$, and crosses the $x$-axis at $x = 1$ and $x = 3$.

### Worked Example:
Sketch the graph of:

$$
f(x) = -x^2 + 2x + 15
$$

Identify the values:

$$
a = -1,\quad b = 2,\quad c = 15
$$

Since $a = -1 < 0$, the graph opens downwards.

Since $c = 15$, the $y$-intercept is $15$.

Find the $x$-intercepts:

$$
-x^2 + 2x + 15 = 0
$$

$$
x^2 - 2x - 15 = 0
$$

$$
(x - 5)(x + 3) = 0
$$

$$
x = 5
$$

or

$$
x = -3
$$

The graph opens downwards, crosses the $y$-axis at $15$, and crosses the $x$-axis at $x = -3$ and $x = 5$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not sketch the graph opening upwards when $a < 0$.
- Do not forget to include the $y$-intercept.
- If there are no $x$-intercepts, use another point on the graph to guide the sketch.

## Concept: Graphs That Do Not Intersect the $x$-Axis
Some quadratic graphs do not intersect the $x$-axis.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- If the graph opens upwards and its minimum point is above the $x$-axis, it does not intersect the $x$-axis.
- If the graph opens downwards and its maximum point is below the $x$-axis, it does not intersect the $x$-axis.
- In this case, use the $y$-intercept and another point to help sketch the graph.

### Worked Example:
Sketch the graph of:

$$
f(x) = x^2 + 1
$$

Identify the values:

$$
a = 1,\quad b = 0,\quad c = 1
$$

Since $a = 1 > 0$, the graph opens upwards.

Since $b = 0$, the $y$-axis is the axis of symmetry.

Since $c = 1$, the $y$-intercept is $1$.

The minimum point is:

$$
(0, 1)
$$

When $x = 2$:

$$
f(2) = 2^2 + 1
$$

$$
= 5
$$

So the graph passes through $(2, 5)$.

### Worked Example:
Sketch the graph of:

$$
f(x) = -x^2 - 3
$$

Identify the values:

$$
a = -1,\quad b = 0,\quad c = -3
$$

Since $a = -1 < 0$, the graph opens downwards.

Since $b = 0$, the $y$-axis is the axis of symmetry.

Since $c = -3$, the $y$-intercept is $-3$.

The maximum point is:

$$
(0, -3)
$$

When $x = 1$:

$$
f(1) = -(1)^2 - 3
$$

$$
= -4
$$

So the graph passes through $(1, -4)$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A quadratic graph does not always cross the $x$-axis.
- When there are no $x$-intercepts, show the turning point and another point clearly.

## Concept: Solving Problems Involving Quadratic Equations
Quadratic equations can be used to solve real-world problems involving measurements, area, cost, speed, and other quantities.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Understand the problem and define the unknown variable.
- Form an expression or equation from the given information.
- Rearrange the equation into quadratic form.
- Solve the quadratic equation.
- Reject values that are not meaningful in the context, such as negative lengths.
- Use the valid value to answer the problem.
- Check the answer by substituting it back into the original condition.

### Worked Example:
Joseph wants to make the framework of a cuboid using wooden rods. The base is a square with side $x$ cm. The height is $(x + 30)$ cm. The total surface area is $4\,800$ cm$^2$. The wooden rod costs RM$5$ per metre, and Joseph has RM$15$.

The total surface area is:

$$
2(x)(x) + 4(x)(x + 30)
$$

$$
= 2x^2 + 4x^2 + 120x
$$

$$
= 6x^2 + 120x
$$

Given the surface area is $4\,800$ cm$^2$:

$$
6x^2 + 120x = 4800
$$

$$
6x^2 + 120x - 4800 = 0
$$

Divide by $6$:

$$
x^2 + 20x - 800 = 0
$$

Factorise:

$$
(x + 40)(x - 20) = 0
$$

$$
x = -40
$$

or

$$
x = 20
$$

Since length cannot be negative, reject $x = -40$.

Therefore:

$$
x = 20
$$

The measurements of the box are $20$ cm by $20$ cm by $50$ cm.

Total length of the edges:

$$
8(20) + 4(50)
$$

$$
= 160 + 200
$$

$$
= 360\text{ cm}
$$

$$
= 3.6\text{ m}
$$

Actual cost:

$$
3.6 \times 5 = 18
$$

The actual cost is RM$18$.

Since RM$18 >$ RM$15$, Joseph does not have enough budget.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Reject negative measurements when the question involves length, width, height, distance, or time.
- Always answer the final question in context, not only solve for $x$.
- Check whether the calculated value satisfies the original condition.
