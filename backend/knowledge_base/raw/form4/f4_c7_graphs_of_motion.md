# Topic: Graphs of Motion
**Form:** Form 4 | **Chapter:** Chapter 7 | **Curriculum:** SPM KSSM

## Overview
Graphs of motion are used to represent the movement of an object using graphs. In this topic, students learn about distance-time graphs and speed-time graphs. A distance-time graph shows how distance changes with time, while a speed-time graph shows how speed changes with time.

## Concept: Distance-Time Graph
A distance-time graph represents the motion of an object using distance and time.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- The vertical axis represents distance.
- The horizontal axis represents time.
- The gradient of the graph represents speed.
- Speed is the rate of change of distance with respect to time.

$$
\text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}
$$

For a distance-time graph:

$$
\text{Gradient} = \frac{\text{Change in distance}}{\text{Change in time}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\text{Gradient} = \text{Speed}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not confuse the vertical and horizontal axes.
- In a distance-time graph, gradient represents speed, not distance.
- A steeper graph means a higher speed.
- A horizontal line means the object is stationary.

## Concept: Drawing Distance-Time Graphs from a Table
A distance-time graph can be drawn using values from a distance-time table.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Choose suitable scales for distance and time.
- Plot the points from the table.
- Join the plotted points using a ruler.
- Label the axes clearly with units.

### Worked Example:
The table shows the distance travelled by Azreen during cycling training.

| Time (minutes) | $0$ | $30$ | $60$ | $90$ | $120$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Distance (km) | $0$ | $10$ | $20$ | $30$ | $40$ |

The points to plot are:

$$
(0,0),\ (30,10),\ (60,20),\ (90,30),\ (120,40)
$$

After plotting the points, join them with a straight line.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Use a suitable scale so that the graph is clear.
- Plot the time on the horizontal axis.
- Plot the distance on the vertical axis.
- Always write the units on both axes.

## Concept: Drawing Distance-Time Graphs from an Equation
A distance-time graph can also be drawn from an equation relating distance and time.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Use the equation to form a table of values.
- Substitute selected values of time into the equation.
- Plot the points.
- Join the points using a ruler.

### Worked Example:
Mr Selva drives from Kuala Lumpur to Kuantan. The distance, $s$ km, travelled in $t$ hours is given by:

$$
s = 80t
$$

Draw the graph for $0 \leq t \leq 3$.

Construct a table:

| Time, $t$ (hours) | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Distance, $s$ (km) | $0$ | $80$ | $160$ | $240$ |

When $t = 0$:

$$
s = 80(0) = 0
$$

When $t = 3$:

$$
s = 80(3) = 240
$$

The graph is a straight line passing through:

$$
(0,0),\ (1,80),\ (2,160),\ (3,240)
$$

### Worked Example:
The distance from the finishing line, $s$ km, after $t$ minutes is given by:

$$
s = 8 - \frac{1}{5}t
$$

for $0 \leq t \leq 40$.

Construct a table:

| Time, $t$ (minutes) | $0$ | $40$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Distance, $s$ (km) | $8$ | $0$ |

When $t = 0$:

$$
s = 8 - \frac{1}{5}(0) = 8
$$

When $t = 40$:

$$
s = 8 - \frac{1}{5}(40) = 0
$$

The graph is a straight line from $(0,8)$ to $(40,0)$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Two points are enough to draw a straight-line graph.
- Substitute time values carefully.
- If the graph decreases, the distance value becomes smaller as time increases.

## Concept: Interpreting Distance-Time Graphs
The shape of a distance-time graph describes the motion of an object.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Positive gradient: the object is moving away from the starting point.
- Zero gradient: the object is stationary.
- Negative gradient: the object is moving back towards the starting point or moving in the opposite direction.
- The gradient of a straight-line segment gives uniform speed.
- Positive and negative speeds indicate opposite directions of motion.

### Interpretation of Common Segments:
| Graph Segment | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Positive gradient | Object moves at uniform speed |
| Horizontal line | Object is stationary |
| Negative gradient | Object moves back towards the starting point |
| Steeper gradient | Greater speed |
| Less steep gradient | Lower speed |

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A horizontal line does not mean the object has no distance; it means the distance does not change.
- Negative gradient in a distance-time graph means motion in the opposite direction.
- The speed is taken as positive when describing magnitude, even if the gradient is negative.

## Concept: Calculating Speed from a Distance-Time Graph
Speed can be calculated using the gradient of the distance-time graph.

**Key Rules / Methods:**

$$
\text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Change in distance}}{\text{Change in time}}
$$

or

$$
\text{Speed} = \frac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}
$$

### Worked Example:
A car travels from $0$ km to $150$ km in $2$ hours.

The speed is:

$$
\text{Speed} = \frac{150 - 0}{2 - 0}
$$

$$
= \frac{150}{2}
$$

$$
= 75\text{ km h}^{-1}
$$

### Worked Example:
A tour bus travels from $150$ km to $0$ km in $3$ hours.

The rate of change in distance is:

$$
\frac{0 - 150}{3 - 0}
$$

$$
= -50\text{ km h}^{-1}
$$

The negative sign shows that the tour bus moves in the opposite direction.

The speed of the tour bus is:

$$
50\text{ km h}^{-1}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Use the corresponding time interval for the graph segment.
- If the graph is a straight line, the speed is the same anywhere on that segment.
- The unit must match the graph, such as km h$^{-1}$ or m s$^{-1}$.
- A negative value indicates direction; speed as a magnitude is positive.

## Concept: Stationary Period in a Distance-Time Graph
An object is stationary when the distance does not change over a period of time.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- A stationary period is represented by a horizontal line.
- The gradient of a horizontal line is $0$.
- The object stops for that duration.

### Worked Example:
A car is stationary from $2$ hours to $3$ hours.

The stationary period is:

$$
3 - 2 = 1
$$

Therefore, the car is stationary for $1$ hour.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Do not say the object is moving slowly when the graph is horizontal.
- A horizontal line means the object is not moving.
- The distance remains constant during a stationary period.

## Concept: Describing Motion from a Distance-Time Graph
A distance-time graph can be used to describe the motion of an object in words.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
When describing motion:
- State whether the object is moving or stationary.
- State the distance travelled.
- State the time taken.
- State the speed if needed.
- Mention if the object moves back towards the starting point.

### Worked Example:
A car travels from $150$ km to $270$ km from the $3$rd hour to the $4.5$th hour.

Distance travelled:

$$
270 - 150 = 120\text{ km}
$$

Time taken:

$$
4.5 - 3 = 1.5\text{ hours}
$$

Speed:

$$
\frac{120}{1.5} = 80\text{ km h}^{-1}
$$

Description:

The car travels $120$ km in the last $90$ minutes with a speed of $80\text{ km h}^{-1}$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Convert $90$ minutes to $1.5$ hours when using km h$^{-1}$.
- Use the correct segment of the graph.
- Describe the motion based on the graph, not by guessing.

## Concept: Average Speed
Average speed is used when speed changes during a journey.

**Key Rules / Methods:**

$$
\text{Average speed} = \frac{\text{Total distance}}{\text{Total time}}
$$

### Worked Example:
A car travels $80$ km in $1.5$ hours.

The average speed is:

$$
\text{Average speed} = \frac{80}{1.5}
$$

$$
= 53\frac{1}{3}\text{ km h}^{-1}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Average speed uses total distance, not displacement.
- Include all parts of the journey, including return journeys.
- If the object goes to a place and returns, total distance includes both directions.

## Concept: Solving Problems Involving Distance-Time Graphs
Distance-time graph problems often involve finding distance, time, speed, average speed, or completing a graph.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Use the formula:

$$
\text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}
$$

- Rearranged formulas:

$$
\text{Distance} = \text{Speed} \times \text{Time}
$$

$$
\text{Time} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Speed}}
$$

- Use the graph to identify given values.
- Complete missing parts by calculating missing distance or time.
- Convert units when necessary.

### Worked Example:
Mr Tan travels from Seremban to Rawang with an average speed of:

$$
66\frac{2}{3}\text{ km h}^{-1}
$$

The time taken is $1.5$ hours.

Distance from Seremban to Rawang:

$$
\text{Distance} = \text{Speed} \times \text{Time}
$$

$$
= 66\frac{2}{3} \times 1.5
$$

$$
= 100\text{ km}
$$

If the total distance from Seremban to Lumut is $300$ km, then the remaining distance is:

$$
300 - 100 = 200\text{ km}
$$

If the speed from Rawang to Lumut is $80\text{ km h}^{-1}$, then:

$$
\text{Time} = \frac{200}{80}
$$

$$
= 2.5\text{ hours}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Use the correct part of the journey.
- Convert minutes to hours when speed is in km h$^{-1}$.
- If the graph has a horizontal segment, include the stationary time in total time.
- Average speed is calculated using the whole journey.

## Concept: Speed-Time Graph
A speed-time graph represents how the speed of an object changes with time.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- The vertical axis represents speed.
- The horizontal axis represents time.
- The gradient of the graph represents acceleration.
- The area under the graph represents distance travelled.

For a speed-time graph:

$$
\text{Acceleration} = \frac{\text{Change of speed}}{\text{Change in time}}
$$

and

$$
\text{Distance travelled} = \text{Area under the graph}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- In a speed-time graph, gradient represents acceleration, not speed.
- In a speed-time graph, area under the graph represents distance.
- Do not use the gradient of a speed-time graph to find distance.

## Concept: Drawing Speed-Time Graphs from a Table
A speed-time graph can be drawn using values from a speed-time table.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Choose suitable scales for speed and time.
- Plot the points from the table.
- Join the points using a ruler.
- Label the axes with correct units.

### Worked Example:
The table shows the speed of a car for $5$ seconds.

| Time (seconds) | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $5$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Speed (m s$^{-1}$) | $0$ | $10$ | $20$ | $30$ | $40$ | $50$ |

The points to plot are:

$$
(0,0),\ (1,10),\ (2,20),\ (3,30),\ (4,40),\ (5,50)
$$

Join the points to obtain the speed-time graph.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Time is plotted on the horizontal axis.
- Speed is plotted on the vertical axis.
- A rising straight line means speed is increasing uniformly.

## Concept: Drawing Speed-Time Graphs from an Equation
A speed-time graph can be drawn from an equation relating speed and time.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Use the equation to form a table of values.
- Substitute selected values of time.
- Plot the points and join them.

### Worked Example:
The speed, $v$ km h$^{-1}$, of an aeroplane landing is given by:

$$
v = 800 - 1600t
$$

for $0 \leq t \leq 0.5$.

Construct a table:

| Time, $t$ (hours) | $0$ | $0.5$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Speed, $v$ (km h$^{-1}$) | $800$ | $0$ |

When $t = 0$:

$$
v = 800 - 1600(0) = 800
$$

When $t = 0.5$:

$$
v = 800 - 1600(0.5) = 0
$$

The graph is a straight line from $(0,800)$ to $(0.5,0)$.

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Use at least two points for a straight-line graph.
- Check the given time interval.
- A decreasing speed-time graph shows deceleration.

## Concept: Area Under a Speed-Time Graph
The area under a speed-time graph represents the distance travelled.

**Key Rules / Methods:**

$$
\text{Distance travelled} = \text{Area under the speed-time graph}
$$

Common area formulas:
- Rectangle:

$$
\text{Area} = \text{length} \times \text{width}
$$

- Triangle:

$$
\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}
$$

- Trapezium:

$$
\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(a+b)h
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are the parallel sides and $h$ is the perpendicular height.

### Worked Example:
A speed-time graph forms a trapezium with time $\frac{4}{5}$ hour and speeds $5\text{ km h}^{-1}$ and $10\text{ km h}^{-1}$.

Distance travelled:

$$
\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{5} \times (5 + 10)
$$

$$
= 6\text{ km}
$$

### Worked Example:
A speed-time graph has a time interval of $6$ minutes and speeds $40\text{ km h}^{-1}$ and $80\text{ km h}^{-1}$.

Convert time to hours:

$$
6\text{ minutes} = \frac{6}{60}\text{ hour}
$$

Distance travelled:

$$
\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{6}{60} \times (40 + 80)
$$

$$
= 6\text{ km}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Make sure the time unit matches the speed unit.
- If speed is in km h$^{-1}$, time must be in hours.
- If speed is in m s$^{-1}$, time must be in seconds.
- Split the graph into rectangles, triangles, or trapeziums if needed.

## Concept: Calculating Average Speed from a Speed-Time Graph
Average speed can be found using the total distance from the area under the graph.

**Key Rules / Methods:**

$$
\text{Average speed} = \frac{\text{Total distance}}{\text{Total time}}
$$

### Worked Example:
A car travels a total distance of $30.27$ km in $36$ minutes.

Convert time to hours:

$$
36\text{ minutes} = \frac{36}{60}\text{ hours}
$$

$$
= 0.6\text{ hours}
$$

Average speed:

$$
\frac{30.27}{0.6}
$$

$$
= 50.45\text{ km h}^{-1}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Find total distance first using area under the graph.
- Convert time units before calculating average speed.
- Average speed is not always the simple average of the starting and ending speeds.

## Concept: Interpreting Speed-Time Graphs
The shape of a speed-time graph describes how the speed changes.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Positive gradient: speed increases.
- Zero gradient: speed is constant.
- Negative gradient: speed decreases.
- A rising line represents acceleration.
- A horizontal line represents uniform speed.
- A falling line represents deceleration.
- The direction of motion remains the same during acceleration or deceleration unless stated otherwise.

### Interpretation of Common Segments:
| Graph Segment | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rising line | Object accelerates |
| Horizontal line | Object moves at uniform speed |
| Falling line | Object decelerates |
| Area under graph | Distance travelled |
| Gradient | Acceleration or deceleration |

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Deceleration does not necessarily mean the object moves backwards.
- A falling speed-time graph means the object is slowing down.
- If speed remains positive, the object is still moving in the same direction.
- Uniform speed means speed is constant, not zero.

## Concept: Acceleration
Acceleration is the rate of change of speed with respect to time.

**Key Rules / Methods:**

$$
\text{Acceleration} = \frac{\text{Change of speed}}{\text{Change in time}}
$$

or

$$
a = \frac{v-u}{t}
$$

where:
- $u$ is the initial speed.
- $v$ is the final speed.
- $t$ is the time taken.

### Worked Example:
A car’s speed increases from $20\text{ m s}^{-1}$ to $40\text{ m s}^{-1}$ in $5$ seconds.

Acceleration:

$$
\frac{40 - 20}{5 - 0}
$$

$$
= \frac{20}{5}
$$

$$
= 4\text{ m s}^{-2}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Acceleration is found from the gradient of a speed-time graph.
- Use change in speed, not total speed.
- The unit for acceleration is usually m s$^{-2}$.

## Concept: Deceleration
Deceleration occurs when speed decreases.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Deceleration is shown by a negative gradient on a speed-time graph.
- The rate of change of speed may be negative.
- Deceleration is usually stated as a positive magnitude.

### Worked Example:
A car slows down from $25\text{ m s}^{-1}$ to $0\text{ m s}^{-1}$ from the $20$th second to the $35$th second.

Rate of change of speed:

$$
\frac{0 - 25}{35 - 20}
$$

$$
= \frac{-25}{15}
$$

$$
= -\frac{5}{3}\text{ m s}^{-2}
$$

The deceleration is:

$$
\frac{5}{3}\text{ m s}^{-2}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A negative acceleration means deceleration.
- When asked for deceleration, give the positive magnitude.
- When asked for rate of change of speed, the answer may be negative.

## Concept: Distance During Uniform Speed
When an object moves at uniform speed, the speed-time graph is horizontal.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Uniform speed means the speed is constant.
- The area under a horizontal speed-time graph is a rectangle.
- Distance travelled is:

$$
\text{Distance} = \text{Speed} \times \text{Time}
$$

### Worked Example:
A car moves at $25\text{ m s}^{-1}$ from the $10$th second to the $20$th second.

Time interval:

$$
20 - 10 = 10\text{ s}
$$

Distance:

$$
25 \times 10 = 250\text{ m}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- A horizontal line in a speed-time graph means uniform speed, not stationary unless the speed is $0$.
- Use the time interval, not the ending time only.

## Concept: Solving Problems Involving Speed-Time Graphs
Speed-time graph problems often involve acceleration, deceleration, distance, average speed, and unknown values.

**Key Rules / Methods:**
- Use gradient to find acceleration or deceleration.
- Use area under the graph to find distance.
- Use average speed formula when needed:

$$
\text{Average speed} = \frac{\text{Total distance}}{\text{Total time}}
$$

- Split complex graphs into simple shapes.
- Use the condition given in the question to form equations.

### Worked Example:
A car’s speed-time graph has:
- Speed $5\text{ m s}^{-1}$ at $0$ seconds.
- Speed $10\text{ m s}^{-1}$ at $4$ seconds.
- Uniform speed $10\text{ m s}^{-1}$ from $4$ seconds to $t$ seconds.
- Final time $14$ seconds.

The distance travelled in the first $4$ seconds is half the distance travelled at uniform speed.

Distance in first $4$ seconds:

$$
\frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times (5 + 10)
$$

$$
= 30\text{ m}
$$

Distance at uniform speed:

$$
(t - 4) \times 10
$$

Given:

$$
30 = \frac{1}{2}[(t - 4) \times 10]
$$

$$
30 = 5(t - 4)
$$

$$
30 = 5t - 20
$$

$$
50 = 5t
$$

$$
t = 10
$$

### Worked Example:
If the acceleration for the last $4$ seconds is $3.5\text{ m s}^{-2}$ and the speed changes from $10\text{ m s}^{-1}$ to $v\text{ m s}^{-1}$:

$$
\frac{v - 10}{14 - 10} = 3.5
$$

$$
\frac{v - 10}{4} = 3.5
$$

$$
v - 10 = 14
$$

$$
v = 24
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- Use area for distance, not gradient.
- Use gradient for acceleration, not distance.
- For trapeziums, remember to add the two parallel sides.
- Check whether the question asks for acceleration, deceleration, distance, or average speed.

## Concept: Comparing Distance-Time Graphs and Speed-Time Graphs
Distance-time graphs and speed-time graphs show different information.

| Feature | Distance-Time Graph | Speed-Time Graph |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical axis | Distance | Speed |
| Horizontal axis | Time | Time |
| Gradient represents | Speed | Acceleration |
| Area under graph represents | Not commonly used in this topic | Distance travelled |
| Horizontal line means | Stationary | Uniform speed |
| Rising line means | Moving away from starting point | Speed increasing |
| Falling line means | Moving back or opposite direction | Speed decreasing |

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- In a distance-time graph, horizontal means stopped.
- In a speed-time graph, horizontal means constant speed.
- In a distance-time graph, gradient gives speed.
- In a speed-time graph, area gives distance.

## Concept: Unit Conversion in Graphs of Motion
Correct unit conversion is important when solving motion graph problems.

**Key Rules / Methods:**

$$
1\text{ hour} = 60\text{ minutes}
$$

$$
1\text{ minute} = \frac{1}{60}\text{ hour}
$$

$$
1\text{ km} = 1000\text{ m}
$$

### Common Conversions:
- $30$ minutes:

$$
30 \div 60 = 0.5\text{ hours}
$$

- $15$ minutes:

$$
15 \div 60 = 0.25\text{ hours}
$$

- $90$ minutes:

$$
90 \div 60 = 1.5\text{ hours}
$$

### Common Mistakes & Exam Tips:
- If speed is in km h$^{-1}$, convert minutes to hours.
- If speed is in m s$^{-1}$, keep time in seconds.
- Do not mix km h$^{-1}$ with minutes without conversion.
